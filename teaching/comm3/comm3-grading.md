# How we grade COMM 3

This is the standing note for grading challenges in COMM 3 with Claude. Read it at the start of any grading session. The first two sections are the same for every challenge. After that, one section per challenge.

## The process

Grading starts in Grade view, at `/comm3/grade?a=<assignment id>`. Every card there has a link to the student's doc, and the columns are the grade. Claude pulls the links off the page, reads every doc against the checklist for that challenge, and sorts the class into four groups: Complete, Complete with a note, Not quite, Incomplete. A doc that is not shared with me is Incomplete. Then Claude drafts a comment for every card in my voice and shows me the whole sort as a table before anything is written. I edit the sort and the comments until they read like me. Then Claude writes the columns and the comments onto the board. I press Release.

Reading the docs needs the browser, not the Drive connector. The connector cannot open a doc shared by link that I have not opened, so Claude loads each doc's `mobilebasic` view in Chrome and reads the page text. Some students share with the wrong account, and the browser shows Access Denied for both my gmail and my SCU account. Those are the Incomplete cards that say the doc is not shared.

## The voice

A grade is a column, and a column is a word. The comment is the message, so it has to sound like me.

Complete gets "Nice work, [first name]." A slip on a Complete is "One note:" and asks for the thing with "I'd like you to," never "go back." Not quite opens with some version of "I see some effort, [name], but we have some work to do," then names the things, and the opener varies so no two students get the same sentence. Say "we need," not "I need." Keep it positive. Incomplete gives credit first, something specific from what they did do, then what is missing, and makes no promise about what happens next.

If they left my instructions in the doc, the work can still be Complete this week, and they get this line: "One thing I'd like you to do for all future assignments: please delete all my instructions so that your answers are readable and accessible."

The coach's voice, when the comment comes from the recording. Andrew, Oct 19, 2026: "you have to be more encouraging and softer ... make it more as an encouraging coach, and then take that style forever." His verbal notes are blunt ("We don't do minutes in a bar graph," "sounds very AI to me," "doesn't mean anything to us yet"), and written down as said they land as a scolding on a student reading alone. So: keep every point he made, and change how it lands. Each point under What to work on opens with what the student is going for or what is already there, then the ask in his forward phrases, "I'd encourage you to," "I'd like to see you," "next time I want you to." A rule becomes a direction: "Minutes belong on a map," not "We don't do minutes in a bar graph." A hard read stays honest but says the student can do it: "I want to hear you in this, Grayson." A flat question ("Is that a high cost or a low cost?") stays, followed by the way forward. No praise he did not give; the softening is in the framing, not in added compliments.

## Framing exercise (ex1)

Part 1, for each person: a headline, Entman's four parts named (problem, cause, who's good or bad, how it ended), one moment a transcript wouldn't catch, the follow-up about what the other person left out, and why the headline is built the way it is.

Part 2: six headlines for their actual people, a sentence on why for each, at least one equivalency frame and one emphasis frame labeled, two sent with evidence and a note on how it went.

The lines. Missing labels alone is Complete with a note. Confusing the two frame types, or answering the transcript question as "what I'd cut," is Not quite. A whole part missing, the doc not shared, or the wrong assignment is Incomplete.

The common misses, from the first run on Oct 1, 2026: no equivalency or emphasis labels was the most frequent slip. A few students read "a moment that wouldn't show up in a transcript" as a detail to leave out of the write-up, when it means a laugh, a pause, a tone, someone getting defensive. One student had the two frame types flipped. About a fifth of the class shared the doc with the wrong account.

## Visual language exercise (ex2) and Version 2 (ex3)

The brief, both weeks: a mini visual essay in a Google Doc, 3 photos (two
taken by the student; in week 2, one taken Wednesday or later), 3 graphics
(two made by the student), 150 words max. Version 2 is the same essay revised
with what we did in class; anything can be kept, replaced or reshot.

These are pictures, so Andrew looks at every one himself, in the walk on
Grade view, and says how he feels about it. Claude's job before that is the
sweep: open every file in Chrome, say which ones open, count the images and
the words, and flag the odd ones. Then Claude writes the comments from what
Andrew said, in his voice: positive, encouraging, forward-looking, "I'd
encourage you to" and "next time I want you to."

What the first sweep found, Oct 14, 2026:

- 32 of 55 students revised in the same doc they turned in for Version 1, so
  the Version 1 link now shows Version 2. Version 1 is only visible for the
  other 23, or in the doc's version history.
- Canva pages cannot be read or screenshotted by the browser tool. Six
  Version 2 files are canva.link addresses; they open in their own tab from
  the card.
- The mobilebasic image count runs about one high (the page's own icon),
  so "6 images" reads as three photos and three graphics.
- Shared-with-the-wrong-account was rarer than in week 1: two Version 2
  docs and one Version 1 doc came back Access Denied.

How Version 1 was graded, Oct 16, 2026: on completion, not on quality.
Version 2 was the same essay revised, Andrew had looked at every one and
said how he felt, and for 32 students Version 1 survives only in version
history. So every Version 1 link that opens for him is Complete with no
comment, a doc he cannot open is Not submitted with the access note, and
nothing turned in stays Not submitted. His words: "we do it, but with no
comments, except to those who i couldn't access." The access check ran

batches from a docs.google.com tab; a loop of 57 one after another runs
past the tool's 45 second limit. Two were shut: Grayson Dangleis's doc and
