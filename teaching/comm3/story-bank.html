Week 1Framing and identifying stories

The question this week: who is telling this, where did they start, and what did that choice do to the story? Also, what makes something a story at all.

1.1 The Katrina photo captions 2005

What happened. Two news photographs from flooded New Orleans ran within a day of each other. One, from the Associated Press, showed a young Black man wading through water with a bag and was captioned as looting a grocery store. The other, from Agence France-Presse, showed two white residents in almost identical water with almost identical bags and was captioned as finding food. Both wire services defended their wording. The pairing spread online within days and became one of the most cited examples of racial framing in American journalism.

Why it teaches this. This is the entire concept of framing delivered in two sentences and two images, with no context required. Students see it instantly. It also introduces the harder idea underneath: neither photographer lied, and neither caption is factually disprovable on its face. The frame did the work, not the falsehood.

The move. Put both images up with captions covered. Ask what is happening in each. Then reveal the captions.

1.2 Fyre Festival, told twice 2017 to 2019

What happened. A luxury music festival in the Bahamas was marketed with supermodels and private jets, sold thousands of tickets, and collapsed on arrival. Attendees found disaster relief tents and cheese sandwiches. Two documentaries came out four days apart in January 2019. Netflix made one, and it was co-produced by the marketing agency that had made the original promotional video. Hulu made the other, and it paid the festival's founder for an interview. Each film accuses the other of a conflict of interest.

Why it teaches this. The rarest thing in media studies is a controlled experiment, and this is one. Same events, same interview subjects in some cases, same footage in some cases, two films, two villains. It is impossible to watch both and still believe a documentary is just a record of what happened.

The move. Play the two trailers back to back. Ask who the villain is in each and how they know.

1.3 Covington Catholic 2019politically live

What happened. A short video from the steps of the Lincoln Memorial showed a white teenager in a red hat standing close to and apparently smirking at an older Native American man beating a drum. It went everywhere within hours. National outlets, celebrities, and the students' own school condemned them. Then longer footage surfaced showing roughly two hours of context, including a third group that had been shouting at the students first. Much of the coverage reversed. Lawsuits followed.

Why it teaches this. Framing is not only about word choice. It is about where you start and stop. The clip was not doctored. It was cropped in time. Students who have grown up on short-form video need to understand that a cut is an argument.

The move. Show the 60 second version. Take a vote on what happened. Show the long version. Take the vote again.

1.4 Framing Britney Spears 2021

What happened. A New York Times documentary revisited two decades of coverage of Britney Spears, including the interviews where adult men asked a teenager about her virginity, the paparazzi economy built around her breakdown, and the conservatorship that controlled her life and finances from 2008. The film is largely built out of archival footage that was originally broadcast as ordinary entertainment news. Public pressure after its release contributed to the conservatorship ending later that year.

Why it teaches this. It is a documentary whose subject is coverage itself, which makes it a free lesson. Students watch journalists and hosts behaving in ways that were completely normal at the time and are indefensible now. That gap is where framing lives.

The move. Play a two minute archival interview clip cold, with no setup. Ask what they notice. Then tell them it aired in prime time and nobody objected.

1.5 Tonya Harding and Nancy Kerrigan 1994, retold 2014 and 2017

What happened. Weeks before the Winter Olympics, figure skater Nancy Kerrigan was clubbed in the knee by a man hired by people connected to her rival Tonya Harding's ex-husband. Harding denied advance knowledge, pleaded guilty to hindering the investigation, and was banned for life. In 1994 she was covered as a villain and as trash. A 2014 ESPN documentary and the 2017 film I, Tonya retold the same events as a story about class, abuse, and a press corps that had decided who the bad one was before the facts landed.

Why it teaches this. Same facts, two decades apart, opposite sympathies. The retelling did not uncover much new information. It changed the frame from crime story to class story, and that was enough.

The move. Show 1994 coverage, then the 2017 trailer. Ask what new fact caused the change. There is not much of one.

1.6 The McDonald's hot coffee case 1994

What happened. A 79 year old woman named Stella Liebeck spilled McDonald's coffee in her lap and sued. The case became a national punchline about frivolous lawsuits and was cited for years in campaigns for legal reform. The details rarely made it into the joke: she suffered third degree burns requiring skin grafts, the coffee was held at a temperature well above what other restaurants used, McDonald's had hundreds of prior burn complaints on file, and she initially asked only for her medical costs. A 2011 documentary called Hot Coffee laid out how the joke version got built and who funded its spread.

Why it teaches this. Most students arrive knowing the punchline and none of the case. That gap is the lesson. It also introduces the idea that a frame can be deliberately manufactured and paid for, which sets up week seven.

The move. Ask who has heard of the woman who sued over hot coffee. Ask what they know. Write it on the board. Then go through the record.

1.7 The O.J. Simpson Time cover 1994violence, abuse or death is the subject

What happened. Time and Newsweek both put Simpson's police booking photograph on the cover of the same week's issue. Newsweek ran it unaltered. Time commissioned an illustrator to rework it, and the resulting image was noticeably darker and more shadowed. Because the two magazines sat side by side on newsstands, the difference was impossible to miss. Time's managing editor published a response defending the choice as artistic interpretation.

Why it teaches this. It is framing in a single visual variable, with the control group sitting right next to it. Nothing was added or removed from the photograph. Only the treatment changed, and the meaning changed with it.

The move. Show the two covers with no explanation and ask what is different. Someone will get it in about four seconds.

1.8 The Sycamore Gap tree 2023

What happened. A single sycamore standing in a dip along Hadrian's Wall in northern England, well known from a Kevin Costner film and from countless photographs, was cut down overnight with a chainsaw. It led national news in the UK for days. People left flowers. There was a police investigation, arrests, and eventually convictions. No person was harmed.

Why it teaches this. This is your best case for the question of what even counts as a story. A tree got sustained national coverage and public grief in a week when plenty of people died with no coverage at all. Students have to argue about why, and the answers get at recognizability, symbolism, images, and the fact that a story needs a shape.

The move. Give them the facts with no framing and ask whether this is a story. Make them defend it either way.

1.9 Kony 2012 2012

What happened. A 30 minute video from a small nonprofit called Invisible Children aimed to make Ugandan warlord Joseph Kony famous enough to force his arrest. It hit roughly 100 million views in six days, at the time the fastest spread of any video online. Then came the pushback: Ugandan commentators pointing out Kony had not been in the country for years, questions about the organization's spending, criticism of a white American filmmaker centering his own son in the story. The founder had a public breakdown weeks later. The campaign collapsed.

Why it teaches this. It works in this week as a story about who has standing to tell whose story, which is your power and privilege outcome. It also works in week seven as a distribution case. Use it in whichever week you have more room.

The move. Watch the first eight minutes, which is mostly the filmmaker and his young son. Ask whose story this is.

1.10 The Menendez brothers, 1993 and 2024 1989 onwardviolence, abuse or death is the subject

What happened. Two brothers killed their parents in Beverly Hills in 1989. Their defense argued years of sexual and physical abuse by their father. The first trial ended in hung juries. The second, with much of the abuse evidence excluded, ended in life sentences. Coverage in the 1990s largely painted them as spoiled rich kids who wanted an inheritance, and late night comedy ran with it. A 2024 Netflix drama and a companion documentary retold the case with the abuse claims at the center. Public opinion moved substantially, and their case was reopened.

Why it teaches this. This is the clearest available case of a culture reframing something inside a single lifetime. What changed was not the evidence. It was what the audience was prepared to believe about abuse and about who gets believed.

The move. Compare a 1990s news segment with a 2024 one. Ask what changed in the world, not in the case.

1.11 The Willie Horton ad 1988politically liveviolence, abuse or death is the subjectnew

What happened. During the presidential campaign, an independent group ran a television spot about a Massachusetts furlough program under Governor Michael Dukakis and a man named William Horton, who committed rape and assault while on release. The ad used Horton's mugshot. A separate Bush campaign ad covered the same program without showing him. The imagery, the nickname Willie, and the racial dynamics have been debated ever since. Lee Atwater, who ran the Bush campaign, apologized before his death.

Why it teaches this. A frame built deliberately, tested, and deployed, and it worked. It is the clearest case of framing as a paid professional craft rather than an unconscious bias, and it connects framing to your power and privilege outcome without you having to force the link.

The move. Show the ad, then the official campaign version without the photograph. Ask what the photograph added.

1.12 The same Trump action, framed two ways any given weekpolitically livenew

What happened. Pick one thing the President did in the week before class. A signed order, a firing, a post, a trip. Find how two outlets on opposite sides covered it: the headline, the photograph chosen, the first sentence, and the word used for the action itself. The facts will match. Almost nothing else will. Every outlet involved would say it was reporting accurately.

Why it teaches this. Katrina is the clean version of framing with the politics removed. This is the same lesson with the politics put back in, on a subject every student in the room already has a position on. It works precisely because they will notice the frame on the side they disagree with immediately and struggle to see it on their own side. That struggle is the lesson.

The move. Two headlines side by side, outlets hidden. Ask what happened. Then ask which one is right. Then reveal the outlets and ask again.

1.13 Dukakis in the tank 1988politically livenew

What happened. Trying to counter a perception of weakness on defense, Michael Dukakis staged a photo opportunity riding an M1 Abrams tank in an oversized helmet. The press pool laughed. The Bush campaign put the footage in an attack ad within weeks. The event was designed by his own staff to produce favorable images and produced the opposite.

Why it teaches this. A frame that the subject built for himself and lost control of. It pairs with Willie Horton as the other half of the same campaign: one image was weaponized by an opponent, one was self-inflicted. It also teaches that you do not own how your footage gets used.

The move. Ask what the staff wanted this to say. Then ask what went wrong, mechanically, not politically.

Week 2Visual language

Shot choice, angle, distance, movement, and edit. The question: what did the camera decide, and who was holding it?

2.1 The Rodney King tape 1991violence, abuse or death is the subject

What happened. A man named George Holliday filmed Los Angeles police beating a Black motorist from his apartment balcony. The tape ran nationally and seemed unambiguous. At trial, the defense played it frame by frame, slowed and stilled, arguing each individual blow as a reasonable response. The officers were acquitted, and Los Angeles burned.

Why it teaches this. The founding case of citizen video, and also the founding case of what an edit can do to one. The same footage produced two opposite conclusions depending on speed and framing. This is the week's hardest and most important example.

The move. Play it at speed, then slowed. Ask whether slowing something down makes it more true or less.

2.2 The Exxon Valdez otter and bird photographs 1989

What happened. A tanker ran aground in Prince William Sound and spilled roughly 11 million gallons of crude oil. The volume, the coastline mileage, and the cleanup cost were all reported thoroughly. What lodged in public memory was a small number of photographs of oiled sea otters and seabirds. Those images drove legislation.

Why it teaches this. A demonstration that one photograph can outperform every number in the story. Useful for arguing with your own week three material, since it suggests the picture sometimes beats the chart.

The move. Read the statistics aloud first. Then show one photo. Ask which one they will remember next week.

2.3 Astroworld 2021violence, abuse or death is the subject

What happened. A crowd crush at Travis Scott's festival in Houston killed ten people. Because the audience was full of phones, the event was documented from dozens of angles simultaneously, including from inside the crush. Investigators, lawyers, and journalists reconstructed the timeline largely from that footage. The official account and the phone footage did not always agree.

Why it teaches this. Modern events are covered from a hundred amateur cameras at once, and journalism now includes assembling those into a coherent visual account. It shows students that the angle you happen to have determines the story you can tell.

The move. Show a reconstruction that stacks multiple angles. Ask what any single angle would have missed.

2.4 The Tyre Nichols body camera footage 2023violence, abuse or death is the subject

What happened. Memphis police beat Tyre Nichols after a traffic stop, and he died three days later. The city released roughly an hour of footage from four sources: three officer body cameras and a pole mounted street camera. Officials debated the release for weeks. News organizations then had to decide how much to show, whether to warn, and where to cut.

Why it teaches this. Body camera video has its own grammar. It is chest height, it moves with a body, it has terrible framing, and it feels objective precisely because nobody composed it. That feeling is worth interrogating. The release decisions are also a clean editorial ethics case.

The move. Do not play the footage. Discuss the release decision instead, and ask what the pole camera shows that the body cameras cannot.

2.5 The Abu Ghraib photographs 2004violence, abuse or death is the subject

What happened. Photographs of American soldiers abusing detainees at a prison in Iraq were published by CBS and The New Yorker. The critical detail is who took them. They were snapshots made by the perpetrators, some posing and giving a thumbs up, apparently as trophies. They were not documentary images made by a journalist.

Why it teaches this. Authorship changes how an image reads. These photographs are evidence of the act and also evidence of an attitude toward the act, because someone chose to pose. It pushes students past composition into the question of why the camera was there at all.

The move. Describe the images rather than displaying them. Ask what changes when you learn who held the camera.

2.6 The Challenger launch broadcast 1986violence, abuse or death is the subject

What happened. The space shuttle broke apart 73 seconds after launch, live on television, with schoolchildren watching because a teacher was aboard. The broadcast footage is a locked off long lens shot. The camera does not move. Mission control audio continues flatly for several seconds after.

Why it teaches this. A static wide shot with no cut and no commentary is a formal choice, and here it is devastating precisely because nothing intervenes. Useful against the assumption that more coverage means more camera movement.

The move. Watch without commentary. Ask what a modern edit would have done to it, and whether that would be better.

2.7 The Los Angeles fires January 2025

What happened. Wind driven fires destroyed large parts of Pacific Palisades and Altadena. Coverage came simultaneously from news helicopters, consumer drones, residents' phones held out of car windows during evacuation, and doorbell cameras. Each source produced a visually distinct account of the same fire.

Why it teaches this. Recent, close to home, and it lets you compare four camera heights on one event in one class period. Helicopter reads as scale and news authority. Drone reads as cinematic. Phone reads as panic. Doorbell reads as evidence.

The move. Same fire, four heights, in that order. Ask which one makes you feel like you are in it and why that is a craft decision.

2.8 Free Solo 2018

What happened. A documentary following Alex Honnold's ropeless climb of El Capitan. The filmmakers, themselves climbers, discuss on camera the problem that their presence might distract him and that they might be filming a death. They make explicit decisions about camera placement partly on that basis, including keeping operators off certain pitches.

Why it teaches this. The only film on this list where the crew narrates its own shot decisions and the ethics behind them. It turns cinematography from a technical topic into a decision-making topic, which is what you actually want them to learn.

The move. Show the behind the scenes segment before the climbing footage. Reverses the usual order and makes the shots visible as choices.

2.9 The Falling Man 2001violence, abuse or death is the subject

What happened. Richard Drew of the Associated Press photographed a man falling from the North Tower. It ran widely on September 12 and then almost vanished, pulled after complaints that it was exploitative. Esquire published a long piece in 2003 about the photograph, the attempt to identify the man, and why American outlets stopped running it while foreign ones did not.

Why it teaches this. Ties directly to your 9/11 week. It raises the question of what an image is for and who gets to decide it is too much, and it makes visible an editing decision that was made almost invisibly and nationally.

The move. Discuss the withdrawal decision. Ask who made it, on what grounds, and whether anyone voted on it.

2.10 NFL Films house style 1960s onward

What happened. NFL Films built a recognizable visual and audio grammar for football: slow motion at high frame rates, low camera angles, tight lenses on faces and hands, orchestral scoring, and a deep narration voice. Almost every sports highlight package made since borrows from it. The company was for decades run by a father and son, Ed and Steve Sabol, who were explicit that they were making myth rather than reportage.

Why it teaches this. Perfect for you given the sport background, and it is the cleanest example of craft creating meaning. Nothing in a football game is inherently mythic. Frame rate, lens, and score make it so, and you can strip those away in class and watch the myth disappear.

The move. Play an NFL Films clip, then the same kind of play on a raw broadcast feed. Ask what the craft added.

2.11 Evan Vucci's raised fist photograph 2024politically liveviolence, abuse or death is the subjectnew

What happened. An Associated Press photographer covering a rally in Butler, Pennsylvania made an image seconds after gunfire, of Donald Trump with blood on his face, fist raised, surrounded by agents, with the flag above. Vucci has described moving to get a clean angle in the middle of it. It ran worldwide within hours and became a campaign image, a merchandise image, and eventually a statue.

Why it teaches this. Composition under maximum pressure, and every element in it is a craft decision made in about two seconds. Angle, the flag placement, the moment chosen out of a burst. It is also the most recent proof that a single frame can outrun everything written about the same event.

The move. Show several frames from the sequence, not just the famous one. Ask why this one and not the frame before it.

2.12 The Situation Room photograph 2011politically livenew

What happened. White House photographer Pete Souza made an image of President Obama, Hillary Clinton, and staff watching the Bin Laden raid from a small side room. It is cramped, badly lit, and nobody is centered. The President is off to the side in a folding chair. It became one of the most viewed photographs on the internet at the time.

Why it teaches this. The opposite lesson from the Vucci image. Nothing here is composed to standard, and the failures of composition are what make it read as real. Together the two photographs let you argue about whether good visual craft means control or means giving it up.

The move. Put it next to the Vucci photograph. Ask which one is better made, and make them define better.

Charts, maps, diagrams, and data as story. The question: what can a graphic say that a sentence cannot, and when does one lie?

3.1 The Ever Given in the Suez Canal 2021

What happened. A container ship roughly as long as the Empire State Building is tall wedged itself diagonally across the Suez Canal and blocked it for six days. It was simultaneously a global supply chain crisis and one of the most productive memes of the year, largely because of a single photograph of one small excavator next to the enormous hull.

Why it teaches this. The story is unexplainable without graphics. You need scale comparison, a map, and a diagram of trade volume through the canal. It is also funny, which buys you attention for a data lesson.

The move. Try to explain the blockage with words only for one minute. Fail. Then put up the diagram.

3.2 The Baltimore Key Bridge collapse 2024violence, abuse or death is the subject

What happened. A container ship lost power and struck a support pier of the Francis Scott Key Bridge, which collapsed in seconds. Six construction workers on the bridge died. Coverage relied heavily on diagrams showing the ship's path, the pier strike point, and why that particular structure type fails the way it did.

Why it teaches this. A case where the graphic is the explanation and prose is the supplement. Compare how different outlets diagrammed the same collapse and you get a lesson in what to include and what to leave out.

The move. Show three outlets' diagrams of the same event. Ask what each one chose to leave out.

3.3 The Challenger O-ring charts 1986

What happened. Engineers at the contractor Morton Thiokol argued against launching in cold weather because of concern about O-ring seals. They presented data the night before. The charts they used were dense, poorly organized, and did not clearly plot damage against temperature. The launch went ahead. Edward Tufte later used these charts as his central example of a visualization failure with fatal consequences, though other scholars have argued he overstates how much the charts alone decided it.

Why it teaches this. The strongest possible argument that graphic design is not decoration. The data to prevent the launch existed. It was in the room. The chart failed to say it.

The move. Give them the raw damage and temperature numbers and ten minutes to make a chart. Then show what was actually presented.

3.4 Spotify Wrapped 2016 onward

What happened. Every December, Spotify turns a year of a user's listening into a sequence of animated, shareable cards. It is personal data storytelling designed for vertical screens and built to be posted. Competitors have copied it. In recent years it has also drawn complaints when the data felt thin or the design felt off.

Why it teaches this. The one example on this list they have all personally consumed without ever thinking of it as a genre. It is data visualization, narrative sequencing, platform design, and audience psychology in one artifact, and they already have opinions about it.

The move. Ask what makes a Wrapped card work. Then ask them to design one for something that is not music.

3.5 The Theranos charts and demonstrations 2003 to 2018

What happened. A blood testing startup valued at nine billion dollars claimed to run hundreds of tests from a finger prick. It could not. Investor materials, conference slides, and demonstrations were built to persuade. Validation reports were presented with pharmaceutical company logos on them that those companies had not authorized. The company collapsed after Wall Street Journal reporting, and its founder was convicted of fraud.

Why it teaches this. The dark version of this week's skill. Every technique that makes a graphic persuasive can make a false thing look true. Students should see the persuasion machinery working before they build their own.

The move. Show a Theranos pitch visual. Ask what it is claiming and what it is only implying.

3.6 The Boeing 737 MAX door plug 2024

What happened. A panel covering an unused emergency exit blew off an Alaska Airlines 737 MAX 9 at 16,000 feet. Nobody died. Investigators found four bolts had not been reinstalled after work at the factory, and the paperwork for that work could not be located. The story is largely made of maintenance records, assembly diagrams, and a photograph of the hole.

Why it teaches this. A corporate accountability story that lives in documents. Turning a bolt log into something the public can understand is exactly the job. It also shows students that graphics can carry investigative reporting, not just summarize it.

The move. Show the diagram, then the missing bolts finding. Ask how you would visualize an absence.

3.7 Election night maps and the needle 2016 onwardpolitically live

What happened. American election coverage runs on a small set of graphics: the red and blue state map, county level maps, and The New York Times live forecast dial that moves during the night.
