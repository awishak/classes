# Sequences

Named lecture structures. Each sequence is an ordered list of **slots**. Build a lecture by picking a sequence and filling its slots with [seeds](seeds.md).

Shared slot vocabulary lives in the [README](README.md): `opener` `problem` `solution` `visualization` `call-to-action` `example`.

## How to use

1. Pick the sequence that fits the day's goal.
2. For each slot, pull a seed tagged for that slot and the concept you are teaching.
3. Fill any gaps with fresh material, then note new material back into [seeds.md](seeds.md) so it is reusable.

---

## Monroe's Motivated Sequence (the classic)

The reference. Most persuasive structures are a variation of this.

1. `opener` — Attention. Earn the room.
2. `problem` — Need. Show the gap, the tension, the cost of the status quo.
3. `solution` — Satisfaction. Present the concept/method that resolves it.
4. `visualization` — Visualization. Let them feel the world with the solution in it.
5. `call-to-action` — Action. The concrete next move.

## My sequences

Your own named structures. Start with one, name it, define its slots, and reuse it. A good sequence is one you would happily run three weeks in a row.

### Sequence 1 — The Motivated Sequence
My default teaching shape. Monroe's bones, framed for learning instead of persuasion.

1. `opener` — a seed that earns attention and points at today's concept without naming it yet.
2. `problem` — the gap or tension: what doesn't work, what we can't yet explain, why the concept is needed.
3. `solution` — the concept/method itself, taught plainly.
4. `visualization` — make it stick: a story or image where the concept is doing its work.
5. `call-to-action` — what they should now do, try, or notice before next class.
