Sports and Identity Analysis Guide

COMM 172A · 40 pts

Purpose: research and analyze a topic that sits at the intersection of sports and identity. The intersection has to be specific enough to make the reader think, "oh, that's interesting," and something broad like "gender in sports" does not clear that bar. Research subject must be approved before starting.

  1. 2 ptsResearch intersection State it clearly, and explain why it's interesting.
  2. 10 ptsPopular press research 200 to 300 word summary of at least three high-quality popular press articles, long-form analysis rather than quick news pieces, plus a reference list.
  3. 6 ptsVisual media 150 to 200 word summary of at least 30 minutes of TV or similar programming from a reputable source, plus a citation.
  4. 12 ptsAcademic research 200 to 300 word summary of at least three academic articles, plus a reference list.
  5. 5 ptsPersonal conversations 200 to 300 word summary of conversations with at least three thoughtful people you shared the research with, including their analysis, questions, and future directions, plus a list of who you spoke with and relevant identity markers.
  6. 5 ptsSummary 150 to 250 word summary of what the topic is, why it matters, what's interesting, and its effects.

There's actually a good amount of mainstream reporting on this, and once you start looking you realize its not some fringe internet theory, real journalists have been writing about it for years."its" for "it's," planted, and repeated across the document An NPR piece with University of Colorado professor Pat Ferrucci lays out the basic pattern pretty clearly: white athletes get described in terms of intelligence, effort, and leadership, while Black athletes get described in terms of physical strength and natural ability, and Ferrucci points out that none of the individual words are insulting, its the fact that they cluster by race that's the problem (NPR, 2019). What's cool is these three articles all land on the same conclusion from totally different angles, a professor, a journalist doing interviews, and literal AI analysis of broadcast data, so it's not just one person's opinion.

Part 4: Academic Research

There's actually a solid amount of peer reviewed research backing up what the popular press is saying, going back further than I expected. The biggest one is a 2019 computational linguistics study by Merullo and colleagues, who built a dataset of over 1,400 football broadcasts spanning six decades and used NLP models to analyze how announcers talked about players, and even after controlling for stuff like position and game situation, they still found commentators disproportionately attributed intelligence and leadership to white players and athleticism to nonwhite players (Merullo et al., 2019). This one's cool because its not relying on people's gut feelings about bias, its literally running the numbers on a quarter million player mentions. A second study, this one out of the Netherlands, interviewed actual sports media professionals about their own reporting, and found something kind of depressing, most of the journalists acknowledged racial stereotypes exist in sports commentary in general but almost none of them thought they personally did it, which the researchers point out is a pretty big blind spot for people whose entire job is picking the words to describe players (Van Sterkenburg et al., 2021). A third study looked at this from the audience side instead of the broadcaster side, watching English football fans consume game coverage in real time and finding that white fans reproduce a lot of the same racialized "shades of whiteness" framing that they're hearing from commentators (Van Sterkenburg & Walder, 2024). Put together these three kind of surround the whole system, how commentators actually talk, whether they know they're doing it, and how it lands with the people watching at home.

Part 6: Summary

My topic is the way football broadcasters describe Black and white players using different language, even when the players are doing the same things on the field, with white players more often getting "smart" and "leader" type words and Black players more often getting "athletic" and "natural talent" type words. This matters because language shapes perception, and if broadcasters keep associating leadership and intelligence with one race, that effects real decisions down the line, like who gets hired into coaching and front office jobs after their careers end. What makes it interesting is that this isn't some outlier announcer being obviously racist, its a pattern that shows up across decades of broadcasts, gets confirmed by AI analysis of literally millions of words, and even the broadcasters and fans themselves mostly aren't aware they're doing it. The effects go beyond sports too, since a couple people I talked to pointed out the same "smart vs. athletic" framing shows up in classrooms and other parts of life, which means football broadcasts might just be the most visible, most studied version of a much bigger pattern in how our society assigns intelligence and physicality based on race.

What gives this one away

Students really do write "its" and "effects" by accident, so the typos on their own prove nothing. A real rushed student's writing gets worse in the sections they cared about least. This one is uniformly polished with cosmetic damage sprinkled on top, which is a different thing.

The Quiet Bias in the Booth: How Broadcasters Talk Themselves Into Racial Stereotypesa headline where six labeled parts should be

Turn on any football broadcast for long enough and a pattern emerges that most viewers never consciously register: white players get called smart, disciplined, and heady, while Black players get called explosive, gifted, and freakishly athletic. No single comment sounds like an insult. Taken together, decades of them sound like something else entirely.

That pattern is the subject of a growing body of journalism and research into what scholars sometimes call the "brawn versus brains" divide in sports commentary, and the evidence for it is no longer anecdotal. University of Colorado journalism professor Pat Ferrucci, interviewed on NPR, describes the mechanism plainly: broadcasters consistently link white athletes to intelligence, effort, and leadership, while linking Black athletes to physical strength and natural ability, and Asian athletes to intelligent, team-first play (NPR, 2019). None of these descriptors are individually pejorative. The bias lives in the sorting.

The consequences of that sorting are not confined to the booth. In an interview with WBUR's Only A Game, journalist Derrick Z. Jackson argues that broadcasters who consistently code white players as the intellectual ones are, in effect, auditioning them for jobs that come after the playing career ends: coaching staffs and front offices disproportionately draw from the pool of players already perceived as cerebral (WBUR, 2020). A pattern that looks like harmless color commentary on Sunday afternoon becomes, over a career, a quiet gatekeeping mechanism.

For years, this argument rested largely on close reading and lived experience. That changed in 2019, when a team of computational linguists led by Jack Merullo built a dataset of more than 1,400 football broadcasts spanning six decades and ran natural language processing models across roughly 250,000 individual player mentions. Even after statistically controlling for position and game situation, the researchers found that commentators still disproportionately attached "intelligence" and "leadership" language to white players and "athleticism" language to nonwhite players, along with a separate disparity in how often players were addressed by first name rather than last (Merullo et al., 2019). An Andscape feature on the study noted that some of the largest gaps appeared at the quarterback position specifically, historically the position most associated with on-field decision-making (Andscape, 2020).

What makes the pattern so durable may be that the people producing it do not see themselves in it. A 2021 study interviewing ten sports media professionals in the Netherlands found that while nearly all of them acknowledged that racial stereotyping is a real problem in sports broadcasting generally, almost none identified it in their own commentary (Van Sterkenburg et al., 2021). A related 2024 study went a step further, observing English football fans as they watched matches together, and found that white viewers actively reproduced the same racialized framing in their own running commentary, suggesting the bias does not stop at the broadcast booth but travels into the living room (Van Sterkenburg & Walder, 2024).

The historical roots of this pattern are visible in ESPN's Year of the Black Quarterback, a television special tracing the decades in which Black players were formally barred from quarterback, center, and linebacker, the positions considered, in Hall of Famer Warren Moon's words, the "thinking" spots on the field. What once operated as explicit roster policy has since migrated into the more diffuse, harder-to-name territory of broadcast language. The exclusion is no longer written into any rulebook. It survives instead in adjectives.

Conversations with people outside the research literature suggest the pattern is bigger than football.Part 5 folded into one paragraph, with no list of who was spoken to A former high school wide receiver recalled coaches praising his "burst" and "explosiveness" while reserving "high football IQ" for a far less productive white quarterback on the same roster. A middle school teacher noted that the same intelligence-versus-athleticism framing shows up in how teachers describe students, not just how announcers describe athletes. And a club volleyball coach pointed out an unmistakable gap in the existing research: virtually every study on this subject examines men's sports, leaving open the question of whether, or how, the same pattern operates in commentary on women's athletics.

That gap points to where this line of inquiry goes next. The mechanism by which broadcasters assign competence along racial lines is now well documented in men's football, across six decades of data and multiple independent methods. Whether the same mechanism holds for other sports, other genders, and other media entirely, remains one of the more promising unanswered questions in this line of research.

The tell is structural, and it shows up before you read a sentence

The assignment asks for six labeled parts, each hitting a word count, each followed by its own reference list, because that structure is how the professor checks the rubric. A student who hands in a flowing feature with a headline, no part labels, and one shared reference list has stopped answering the assignment and started answering "write something good about this topic." Nobody chasing forty points throws out the structure the points are attached to. Someone only does that when they've handed the framing to a tool that optimizes for good writing, and never checked the result against the instructions.

The second tell is tonal. Nothing hedges. There's no "I didn't totally have an answer for that," no visible thinking in progress anywhere in the piece. Every paragraph resolves cleanly. Real undergraduate writing, even good real undergraduate writing, usually still shows its work a little.

Different disguises, same shortcut

The frat voice hides the shortcut behind personality. The feature hides it behind quality, and in doing so stops resembling student work at all. The bare minimum hides it behind visible restraint, betting that an answer this thin must have been typed by a tired human rather than accepted, unedited, from a machine that could have done far more. None of the three required the student to do the one thing the assignment wanted, which was to sit with three articles, three studies, and three real conversations long enough to have an opinion about how they fit together.
